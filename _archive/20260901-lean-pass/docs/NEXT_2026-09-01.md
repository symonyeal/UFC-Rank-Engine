## 1. The complaint

The published top 100 is ordered by the all-time score. Every board also prints
**Prime** (the fighter's rating in their best ten years) and **Elite wins**
(contenders beaten in those years, where a contender was rated 1,750 or higher at
the time with at least 8 UFC fights of their own). Those two columns frequently
disagreed with the rank beside them, in ways a reader notices immediately:

| # | fighter | score | skill | title | schedule | exposure | title W–L | elite wins | prime |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| 40 | Kamaru Usman | 670.2 | 175.0 | 561.4 | 424.1 | 0.90 | 6–5 | 9 | 1967 |
| 44 | Tyron Woodley | 615.5 | 114.0 | 354.5 | 462.3 | 1.00 | 4–3 | 5 | 1803 |
| 47 | Dricus Du Plessis | 592.2 | 631.8 | 387.2 | 268.6 | 0.80 | 3–2 | 6 | 2043 |
| 60 | Michael Chandler | 475.0 | 216.1 | 527.1 | 225.6 | 0.90 | 5–3 | **2** | 1811 |
| 62 | Sean Strickland | 471.6 | 219.7 | 496.8 | 232.0 | 0.90 | 2–0 | **9** | 1918 |
| 75 | Robert Whittaker | 386.9 | 107.5 | 186.5 | 292.8 | 0.90 | 1–0 | 8 | 1900 |
| 86 | Sean O'Malley | 361.0 | 267.3 | 300.4 | 176.3 | 0.90 | 2–1 | 4 | 1956 |
| 100 | Jiri Prochazka | 310.1 | 163.7 | **61.7** | 242.9 | **0.70** | 2–0 | 4 | 1930 |
| 217 | Sean Brady | 125.2 | 230.1 | **0.0** | 57.0 | 0.80 | 0–0 | 4 | 1968 |

Objections raised: Prochazka at 100 when his losses are title fights; Whittaker
at 75 on 8 elite wins; Strickland (9 elite wins) below Chandler (2); Du Plessis
below Woodley; Usman at 40 on 9 elite wins.

**These are symptoms. The question is whether the score measures what it claims
to, not whether these nine fighters can be reordered.**

## 2. Already measured — start here, do not redo

All figures from dataset `2026-08-13`, published scope, on `main@6561688`.

### 2.1 What actually drove the rank

Across the published top 100, against the final rank:

| component | share of the score | spread | correlation with rank |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| skill | 37.0% | 535.9 | −0.565 |
| title | 35.7% | 509.4 | **−0.721** |
| schedule | 27.3% | 179.1 | **−0.752** |

Schedule was the **smallest** component and the **strongest** predictor of rank.
Skill carried the most weight and predicted rank least. That inversion is the
core finding to explain.

Agreement between the score and elite wins was 0.488, and between the score and
Prime 0.421. The two printed columns explained less than a quarter of the
ordering between them.

### 2.2 The title component is a cliff, not a slope

**14 of the top 100 had a title score of exactly 0.0.** Their median rank was
**86**, against **44.5** for everyone else. Their median elite-win count was 4 —
they were not weak fighters, they were non-champions.

A component carrying 35.7% of the score and correlating −0.721 with rank was zero
for anyone who never won a belt. Demian Maia (7 elite wins), Beneil Dariush (6)
and Anthony Johnson (6) all scored 0.0 there. This was the most likely single
explanation for Whittaker, Strickland-versus-Chandler and Sean Brady.

**Do not assume the cliff is wrong.** A career score may legitimately reward
championships. The question is whether *a hard zero* is the right shape, and
whether the count feeding it is comparable across promotions — see 2.3.

### 2.3 Title fights are 44% non-UFC

Title fights by promotion: UFC 439, Bellator 118, UFC pre-unified 35, ONE 32,
WEC 31, RIZIN 25, Strikeforce 21, PRIDE 13. **The UFC is 56.2% of them.**

Chandler's 5 title wins are Bellator; Strickland's 2 are UFC. Chandler's title
score (527.1) beat Strickland's (496.8) on that count. The résumé prices each win
by the opponent's rating rather than a flat amount per belt, so this is not a raw
counting problem — but opponent ratings inside a thin promotion carry the
inflation described in 2.5, so the pricing inherits it.

### 2.4 The exposure factor

Range across the top 100: 0.54 to 0.97, median 0.86, correlation with rank
−0.286. It multiplies **both** skill and schedule. Prochazka sat at 0.70 and
Woodley at 1.00 — a 30% swing on two of three components before any fight
evidence is considered. It is a hand-typed promotion table, not a measured
quantity. Removing it was already tried — see §4.

### 2.5 The rating problem underneath everything

An undefeated record is rated above every opponent it beat, because nothing caps
the whole-career fit from above. Measured across 20 fighters: those averaging
about 0.8 career losses sat **+79.7** above the best opponent they ever faced;
those averaging about 4.1 losses sat **−68.1**. Shaidullaev is +302, Khabib +206.

Any component built from opponent ratings inherits this — the title résumé, the
schedule component, and the contender bar itself.

### 2.6 Fighters with a strong elite record ranked low

Of the 45 fighters anywhere on the board with 6 or more elite wins, 5 sat outside
the top 100: Glover Teixeira (128), Alexander Volkov (142), Jan Blachowicz (143),
Nassourdine Imavov (241), and **Neil Magny (600, on 6 elite wins, title 0.0,
schedule 23.4)**. Magny is the extreme case worth explaining in full.

## 3. The work

### 3.1 FIRST — decide what the all-time score is for

The board is titled "what did the fighter's career amount to". Two incompatible
readings were mixed into one number:

- **Achievement**: belts won and defended. This justifies the title cliff and
  makes Chandler-above-Strickland correct.
- **Quality of work**: who you beat and how good you were. This makes the cliff
  indefensible and Whittaker badly underrated.

**Write the answer down before changing any weight.** If the score measures
achievement, the elite columns beside it are decoration and the two will never
agree — in which case say so on the page and stop. If it measures quality, the
title component needs a shape that does not zero a non-champion.

### 3.2 Break the disagreement down properly

For each of the 100 published fighters, compute the rank they would hold under
each component alone, and under elite-win mass alone, and report the differences.
The named cases in §1 are the ones to explain, but a fix has to be argued on the
whole distribution, not on nine names.

Specifically test whether the schedule component is doing the work the title
component is credited with. It has the highest rank correlation (−0.752) and the
smallest spread, which is the signature of a tiebreak running across the whole
board rather than a real measurement.

### 3.3 Test the title cliff directly

Three candidate shapes, all cheap:

1. **Keep the zero**, and argue it from the score's stated purpose.
2. **Give a floor for reaching a title fight**, so losing one scores above never
   contesting one. Prochazka's losses being title fights is the case this
   addresses.
3. **Replace the count with the elite-win figure already computed**, and treat
   belts as a multiplier on it rather than a separate added term.

Report for each: movement of the nine named fighters, the count of top-100
fighters at exactly 0.0, and agreement with the three outside lists **with the
confidence interval** — and expect the lists not to settle it, per §4.

### 3.4 Decide the promotion question, with a measurement

Establish whether a Bellator title win and a UFC title win should price equally
once the opponent's own rating is accounted for. The measured pool gap (+54 Elo,
[+10, +100], held out) is the quantity in this neighbourhood and is already used
on the title path. Either extend it, or state why it does not apply.

## 4. Tested and rejected — do not propose these again

- **Removing the exposure factor.** Correlation with UFC fight count falls
  +0.483 → +0.345, but fighters with no UFC record in the top 100 double (5 → 9)
  and Patricio Freire returns to 14th. All three versions hold 37 listed fighters
  in the top 100, so the outside check cannot separate them.
- **A hard cut-off at the contender bar in the title résumé.** It zeroed the
  title component for 38 fighters with three or more title wins (Shevchenko
  11 → 0, Usman 6 → 0) and put Matt Serra 50th all-time on a 7–7 record.
- **Splitting the quality résumé on title status.** Five versions tried; every
  one raised Freire and wrecked agreement with the outside lists.
- **Swapping the title résumé to fight-by-fight ratings.** Hughes 427.4 → 444.7,
  agreement 0.9149 across 245 fighters. Replaces the timing problem with a
  different one.
- **Putting the title bar on the division contender line.** Made the board worse:
  St-Pierre's title résumé fell to 279 against Namajunas's 657, and Namajunas
  reached 9th all-time. A higher bar reads the rating error *more* sensitively,
  not less.
- **Raising `WHR_VIRTUAL_GAMES`.** Rejected twice; the predictive fit moved it
  down, 2.0 → 1.0.
- **Using the outside lists to judge a small change.** Ten to thirty-four
  hand-picked names cannot settle a 54-Elo correction. Use them to catch a
  *large* reordering, never to choose a setting.
- **Ranking the Prime board by rating alone** (Topuria with 5 wins above
  St-Pierre with 11), **by opponent strength ignoring results** (Roy Nelson 0–10
  above Khabib and St-Pierre), or **by evidence-discounted rating** (it flattens;
  Silva and Nemkov end 1.2 points apart). Settled: elite-win mass.

## 5. How a change gets accepted

This is a score change, so prediction error cannot judge it — the score never
predicts a fight. Argue from mechanism, then report:

- movement of all nine fighters in §1, plus Magny, Maia, Dariush and Anthony
  Johnson;
- the count of top-100 fighters with a zero title component, before and after;
- agreement between the score and elite wins, and between the score and Prime,
  currently 0.488 and 0.421;
- agreement with all three outside lists, with the confidence interval and a
  statement of how much that check can actually detect.

State unresolved results as unresolved. An earlier assessment — including
everything in this document — is handed to you to attack.

# OUTCOME — closed 2026-09-01

Full findings: `Claude Status Reports\All-time score vs the elite record —
findings 2026-09-01.md`. Working material and every version scored:
`Claude Func Folder\ufc-rank-engine\py\`.

## §2.1 of this brief was wrong

Its three shares (37.0 / 35.7 / 27.3) are the components' **raw** totals, taken
before each is divided by its own maximum. On the scale that actually enters the
score: skill **21.9%**, title **25.1%**, schedule **53.0%**. There was no
inversion. Schedule was the largest by weight *and* the strongest by rank
correlation, and the board changed its own definition at about rank 25 — the
shares run 36.6 / 38.9 / 24.5 in ranks 1–25 against 12.2 / 29.1 / **58.7** in
ranks 26–100.

The cause: the old code divided by a maximum — a single fighter's value — and the
three components have very different shapes. Their max-to-median ratios are 10.6
for skill, 8.3 for title and **2.9** for schedule, so the flattest component won.
52% of the top 100 sat below a tenth of skill's maximum and 43% below a tenth of
title's, against 3% for schedule. The claim that this combined the three "without
an exchange rate anyone had to invent" was false: the rate was set by whichever
career happened to top each column.

## The schedule component's bar was undocumented and not comparable

`RANK_CONTEXT_TOP_SHARE = 0.20` was adopted on 2026-08-26 against a table of
median division sizes of 37–85. On the published data those divisions are
**144–1,435**, so 20% of the division is more than 15 fighters on **92.7%** of
appearances, and the rule changed the window on only 7.0% — almost all
catchweight rows. Fifteen places is the top 1.05% of lightweight against 2.88% of
light heavyweight. Only 3.93% of wins scored any credit at all; 42.9% of wins
over a contender scored zero, and 77.8% of the wins it did credit were below the
contender bar. Worst of all, it correlated **+0.360 with elite LOSSES** and
**+0.013 with elite wins minus losses** — it was rewarding gatekeeping, the exact
failure this project had already rejected for the Prime board, sitting inside the
majority of the published score.

## What shipped

The max-based normaliser was replaced by dividing each component by the mean of
its own top 100. The rank-context schedule component was replaced by a contender
résumé, screened on the board's own contender line (rated 1,750 or higher with 8
or more UFC fights) and priced against it, with the opponent's rating read from
before the fight and at most one contribution per active year. The stated
weights are achievement 0.30 and quality 0.70, both set by the project owner,
both swept in the code beside the constant.

Acceptance, measured over a fixed group of fighters: agreement with elite wins
0.519 → **0.730**, with Prime 0.418 → **0.632**, named non-anchors in the top 100
6 → **2**, outside-list-only fighters 4 → **2**, watch-list names outside the top
40 two → **none**. The 100 Greatest +0.418 → +0.580 (a change of +0.162, CI95
[−0.009, +0.364]); every other list's interval straddles zero. §4 was right — the
lists selected none of this, and none of it was selected on them.

## §3.4 answered: no further promotion term needed

After the +54 correction, a UFC title win prices at an average weight of 0.119
against Bellator's 0.059 (difference +0.061, CI95 [+0.044, +0.078]). The discount
is present and working through the opponent's rating.

## Still open — carried forward

1. **The title cliff is untouched**, and the zero-title count rose 14 → 15. The
   two candidate shapes were both built and measured; neither was selected.
2. **Jiri Prochazka moved 100 → 139, and no weighting reaches him** (139 at
   achievement weight 0.30, 140 at 0.50). His title record is inverted: both
   title wins are over opponents *below* their division's bar (Lawal 1679 against
   a bar of 1827, weight 0.008; Teixeira 1855 against 1868, weight 0.054) while
   all three title losses are against opponents above it (0.229 / 0.215 / 0.206).
   His losses are worth ten times his wins, and the résumé counts only wins.
   Compounding it, 19 of his 40 rated fights carry no promotion label and fall to
   the lowest tier factor of 0.20, giving him an exposure factor of 0.730 against
   a top-100 median of 0.87. Forcing it to 1.0 moves him to 110.
3. **NEW — 101 duplicate fight rows.** His UFC 275 win over Teixeira appears on
   both 2022-06-11 and 2022-06-12. Across the data there are 101 same-pair rows
   within two days of each other (0.13% of 80,697), all from the `majors` source,
   all used by the model, none excluded — consistent with an international event
   crossing a date boundary and the merge failing to collapse the second row. The
   effect on the résumé is small (five fighters lose one contender win each) but
   **these rows also enter the rating itself**. The fix belongs in the data
   merge, not in any board.
4. **Matt Serra moved 77 → 60 on one contender win**, and rises steadily as the
   achievement weight rises (60 / 49 / 43 / 40 at 0.30 / 0.40 / 0.50 / 0.60). The
   single-upset artefact §4 warns about is still reachable by raising that weight.
5. **The hand-typed promotion table is untouched** and still multiplies both
   quality components.
6. **Two of three bars unified, not three.** The résumé now uses the absolute
   contender line; the title résumé still uses a division-year quantile.
   Unifying them was measured and reverted on 2026-08-26, for reasons that hold.
7. **The career-wide contender-win count and the printed Elite-wins column are
   different things** — one covers the whole career, the other the best ten
   years — so they legitimately differ (Jones 16 against 12). The résumé prices
   the opponent before the fight and the printed column reads the value at the
   event, so they can also differ by one fight at the margin.

The combined table goes 82,171 → 82,072, rated fights 80,697 → **80,598**,

## Carried-forward item 1: both title shapes re-measured, both REJECTED

Measured on the current two-term score over the fixed group; the harness
reproduces the live score and the live title figures exactly before measuring
anything.

- **Crediting a title-fight loss** at a quarter or half the win's weight drops
  the cliff 15 → 7 / 6 and moves Prochazka 139 → 129 / 121, but raises **Sean
  Strickland 20 → 19 → 17**. Strickland is this project's named symptom of
  rewarding exposure rather than contention, and the component retired earlier
  the same day correlated +0.360 with elite losses. Paying for reaching a title
  fight and losing it is that component under a new name. Agreement with elite
  wins +0.013/+0.018, with Prime **−0.009/−0.019**.
- **Elite-win mass with belts as a multiplier** shows the largest numbers in the
  table (elite wins 0.730 → 0.816, Prime 0.632 → 0.767) and they are
  **circular**: this version's title term *is* elite wins multiplied by rating
  above a bar, and those two figures are exactly what the acceptance statistics
  measure. It is being scored against its own inputs. Every outside list is
  unmoved. It also breaks the term it replaces — Matt Serra, a UFC champion,
  scores **0.0**, while Neil Magny, who never fought for a title, scores 50.2 —
  and it counts the résumé twice.

All sixteen list intervals straddle zero. The 100 Greatest is the only list with
real power and both shapes are flat to slightly negative on it.

**The cliff stays open and unchanged at 15.** Note for the next pass: 0.0 for a
fighter who never won a title is what a championship term *should* say. The case
that is genuinely wrong is item 2 — a former UFC champion pricing at 0.008 and
0.054 because his title opponents sat below their division's bar. That is the
title pricing, not the cliff.

## §3.4 measured (still not fixed)

Both problems confirmed by reading the code. The stale window is live in the
model fit, not just the board. Magnitude across 161,196 appearances: the
rank-context factor does nothing on **94.02%** of them and is the single largest
of four factors on **3.28%**. Removing it entirely — the most any change to it
could do — moves the combined figure on those rows by a median of 0.022. The
clip to fifteen places binds on **92.82%** of rows with a known division size.
**New: the division size is unknown on 47.64% of appearances**, so the share rule
cannot fire on nearly half the table whatever its value. Net ceiling on repairing
it: it changes window membership on 2,830 rows and is the largest factor on 1,214
of them — **0.75% of all appearances**. Budget the re-rate against that ceiling.

## New: the boards print a Prime rank column

The all-time boards carry each fighter's position on the published Prime board of
their own gender, read from the written board file so the two tables cannot
disagree. 62 of the men's top 100 are covered, at ranks 1–68 of that board's 70
qualifiers; a blank is that board's abstention. It is deliberately not in step
with the Prime rating beside it (the board orders elite-win mass, Prime is a
rate) and both documents now say so. The ungated Prime board was not used despite
covering all 100: it was retired from publication on purpose, and a rank from an
unpublished board is one the reader cannot check.

Two stale descriptions were corrected in the same paragraphs — `RANKINGS.md`
documented a **Peak** column that had been retired, and both documents still
called the score "schedule strength" after the contender résumé replaced that
component.
